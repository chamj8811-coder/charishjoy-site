A Ticket for One

Sometimes a small journey is simply a way to remember you still have choices.

November 16, 2026CHARISH JOY

The ticket machine asked how many passengers.

She pressed one.

An ordinary number. She had bought single tickets before, for errands and work and appointments. But this time, when the machine printed the receipt, she stood there looking at it as though it had given her permission for something.

She had not told anyone she was going to the sea.

She had mentioned wanting to see it months ago. The idea had been postponed over and over—bad weather, busy weekends, a household list that seemed to grow whenever she looked away. Eventually, she stopped bringing it up.

On Friday evening, she found the old photograph of the coastline she had saved on her phone. Then she checked the train schedule. There was an early departure on Saturday.

She booked it before she could invent a reason not to.

At the station, she bought coffee that was too hot to drink and a sandwich she didn't particularly want. A child beside the departure board was counting the trains aloud, losing track every time an announcement interrupted him.

When her platform appeared, she followed the signs and found her seat beside the window.

No one asked where she was going. No one needed her to remember a charger or hold a bag or decide what they should eat later.

At first, the freedom made her strangely restless.

She checked her phone. No messages. She put it in her coat pocket, took it out again, and finally placed it face down on the empty seat beside her.

The train crossed the edge of the city, past warehouses and low rooftops. She watched laundry flutter on a balcony, then a small field, then a row of houses with blue water tanks.

For the first hour, she thought about everything she should have been doing instead.

Then the landscape opened and she saw the sea.

She almost missed it. There was no grand arrival, no music rising at precisely the right moment. Just a sudden silver line between two buildings. She leaned toward the window, wanting another glimpse.

By the time she reached the shore, the wind had made a mess of her hair. She walked along the promenade until she found a bakery with fogged windows and three tables.

The woman behind the counter asked whether she wanted a seat for two.

“Just me,” she answered.

She expected to feel embarrassed. Instead, she noticed how tired her feet were.

She ordered soup and bread and sat facing the window. Outside, a man was trying to photograph a small dog that refused to sit still. Two friends laughed over something on a phone. Farther down, someone had left a red umbrella open against a bench, even though it wasn't raining.

She wanted to tell somebody about the dog. She reached for her phone, then stopped.

Not because she had nobody to tell. There were people she loved, people who would have listened. But for once, she wanted to keep the moment exactly as it was before turning it into a message.

She stayed longer than she meant to.

After lunch, she walked to the end of the pier. The wind was colder there. Her hands ached inside her pockets, and her coffee had long since gone cold.

She thought she would feel transformed by the trip. Braver, perhaps. Clearer about the decisions waiting for her at home.

She didn't.

She still had an overflowing laundry basket, unanswered questions, and a Monday morning that would arrive whether she was ready or not.

But she had come all this way because she wanted to. The thought was small enough to fit inside a single breath.

Before leaving, she asked a stranger to take a photograph of her by the railing.

In the picture, her hair covered half her face. The horizon tilted. She wasn't smiling.

She kept it.

On the train home, the ticket inspector glanced at her pass and moved on. Beyond the glass, the coastline disappeared into the early dark.

She opened her calendar and looked at the empty squares ahead. For a moment she considered filling one with another destination.

Instead, she left it blank.

She liked knowing she could decide later.

An original work of fiction. The narrator and events are imagined, not an account of the artist’s personal life.
