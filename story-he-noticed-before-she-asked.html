He Noticed Before She Asked

An original fictional short story by CHARISH JOY

October 18, 20267 min read

Part One: The Video

Five months after we broke up, I saw Daniel holding another woman’s hair away from her birthday candles.

It was a Tuesday night in March. I was scrolling through Facebook in bed when a mutual friend’s post appeared.

Priya had uploaded an album.

Happy birthday to the loveliest girl!

There were photographs from a restaurant with warm lighting and mismatched chairs. I almost scrolled past. Then I recognized the gray sweater Daniel used to lend me.

He sat beside a woman with dark curls, his arm resting across the back of her chair.

He looked well.

I hadn’t expected that to hurt.

We had broken up in October. By March, I had stopped expecting his name to appear on my phone. I could mention him without losing the rest of my sentence.

Still, I wasn’t prepared to see him like this.

I swiped.

In one photograph, she laughed against his shoulder. In another, six people raised their drinks toward the camera.

Daniel was looking at her.

The last item was a nineteen-second video.

A small cake sat on the table. Everyone sang. When she leaned forward, her hair slipped over her shoulder toward the candles.

Daniel gathered it gently and held it back.

She closed her eyes to make a wish.

He waited.

I turned the volume down, although I was alone.

Then I watched it again.

Part Two: Last April

My last birthday with Daniel had been the previous April, almost a year earlier.

That morning, he sent a message while I was getting ready for work.

Happy birthday, love. Dinner at seven. See you tonight.

He had chosen the restaurant. I carried that small fact through the day like a promise.

On my way home, I stopped at a bakery near the station and bought a vanilla cake with raspberries.

“Want something written on it?” the woman asked.

“Just the cake, please.”

I thought we might share it after dinner. At home, I put it in the fridge and sent him a message.

Got us a little cake for later.

He replied with a heart.

I wore the green dress he had once said made my eyes look different.

At the restaurant, I took the seat facing the door.

He arrived twenty minutes late, kissed my forehead, and sat down.

“Sorry. Traffic.”

“It’s okay.”

We ordered. He told me about a difficult client, a delayed project, a coworker who had taken credit for his work.

I asked questions. He answered.

When the waiter cleared our plates, Daniel checked his watch.

“Do you want to come up for cake afterward?” I asked.

He rubbed a hand over his face.

“I’m exhausted. Big day tomorrow.”

“Just for a little while?”

“Can we do it at the weekend?”

I looked down at the table.

He noticed.

“What?”

“I thought we’d spend the evening together.”

“We are spending it together.”

“I mean after dinner.”

He leaned back.

“I booked us a nice place. I’m here. What else did you want me to do?”

The waiter approached, saw our faces, and moved to another table.

I could have answered. I had answers.

Stay a little longer. Ask about my day. Let me feel that you wanted to be here.

Instead, I said, “Forget it.”

Daniel sighed.

“Please don’t be upset.”

I watched him for a moment, then made myself smile.

Later, a couple nearby asked their waiter to take a photograph. They moved their chairs together, laughing at something I couldn’t hear.

When our waiter passed, I held out my phone.

“Could you take one of us too?”

“Of course.”

Daniel was typing. I touched his wrist.

He nodded without looking up.

The waiter stood beside us with my camera open.

“Babe,” I said quietly.

Daniel finished his message and put his phone away. He moved his chair beside mine and leaned toward me. I rested my hand on his arm.

The photograph turned out well.

What you couldn’t see was how long we’d waited for him to look up.

I posted it before we left.

My favorite person.

Daniel liked it while we waited for his car.

Outside my building, he kissed me goodbye.

“We’re okay?” he asked.

I was tired of trying to rescue the evening.

“Yeah,” I said.

Upstairs, I took the cake out of the fridge.

The bakery had included candles. I pushed one into the icing and searched the kitchen drawer for a lighter.

There wasn’t one.

I checked behind the stove. Opened the drawer again.

For a moment, I considered knocking on my neighbor’s door.

Then I pulled the candle out and cut myself a slice.

I ate it standing at the counter, still wearing the green dress.

It was a very good cake.

Part Three: The Message

The Facebook video ended.

I returned to the photograph where Daniel was looking at his girlfriend and enlarged it. Then I closed it, embarrassed by how closely I was studying his face.

A photograph couldn’t tell me what their relationship was like.

I knew that. I had posted a convincing one myself.

But I kept returning to his hand.

She hadn’t needed to interrupt the singing or touch his wrist. He had been watching. He had noticed.

I opened our old conversation.

The last messages were from October, shortly after the breakup.

A jacket. A charger. Arrangements for returning my keys.

Below them, I typed:

I ate my birthday cake alone that night. I don’t think I ever told you that.

My thumb hovered over Send.

Perhaps he would apologize.

Perhaps he wouldn’t remember the evening the way I did.

I imagined explaining why I was writing about a birthday from nearly a year ago. I would tell him about the video, then reassure him that I wasn’t trying to interfere with his new relationship.

I began rehearsing the rest.

I know you were tired. I wasn’t expecting anything expensive. I just thought—

I stopped.

I had asked him to stay. I remembered that clearly now.

I looked at the message again.

I didn’t want our first conversation in months to begin with me trying to make that night sound painful enough to matter.

I deleted it and closed the thread.

A few minutes later, I picked up my phone again.

I checked the time and put it back.

Over the following weeks, the video came to mind at inconvenient moments: waiting for a lift, rinsing a plate, turning over in bed.

Some days, I barely thought about him.

Once, I opened Priya’s profile, then left before reaching the album.

I didn’t contact Daniel.

Part Four: The Reservation

On a Sunday afternoon in late March, nearly three weeks after I had seen the post, my mother called.

My birthday was the following Saturday.

“Have you made plans?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

“You should do something.”

“Maybe dinner.”

“With who?”

“I’ll ask Ines.”

After we hung up, I sent Ines a message.

Are you free for my birthday?

She called immediately. I answered while folding laundry on the couch.

“Dinner,” she said. “My treat.”

“You don’t have to—”

“I know. Where do you want to go?”

I paused with a half-folded towel in my hands.

“Anywhere is fine.”

“Give me an actual restaurant.”

There was a place near the river I had saved the previous summer. Daniel and I had talked about going, but we never had.

I opened the menu.

The prices were higher than I usually chose. I nearly sent Ines the name of somewhere cheaper.

I switched the call to speaker and texted Ines the link to the restaurant by the river.

“Just opening it,” she said.

The line went quiet. I began folding the same towel again while she read the menu on her phone.

Then her voice came through the speaker.

“Oh, this looks gorgeous. Book it.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes. Seven?”

“Seven.”

After we hung up, I opened the reservation page.

Two guests.

Saturday.

Seven o’clock.

Are you celebrating a special occasion?

I selected Birthday.

Another box appeared.

Anything else we should know?

I typed:

Nothing big, please.

My thumb moved toward Confirm.

A message from Ines appeared at the top of the screen.

Booked yet? I’m already choosing dessert.

I read it twice.

Then I deleted Nothing big, please and confirmed the table.

This is a work of fiction. The characters and events are imagined.
