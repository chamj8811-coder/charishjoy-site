Still Here, But Not the Same

Fiction · An original first-person narrative inspired by “Still Here, But Not the Same.”

October 11, 2026Original short fiction

He told me about his Saturday plans while searching for his keys.

“I'm having dinner with some friends tonight,” he said.

I was standing by the kitchen counter, trying to decide what we should have for dinner. I'd been thinking we might go out, maybe somewhere close enough to walk. Nothing special. We hadn't had an evening to ourselves in weeks.

“Oh,” I said. “I thought we might do something tonight.”

He looked up, genuinely surprised.

“You didn't say anything.”

He was right, of course. I hadn't mentioned it. There had been no conversation, no arrangement he'd forgotten, nothing he could reasonably be accused of breaking.

“We can go tomorrow,” he offered.

I told him it was fine and wished him a good evening.

After he left, I stood in the kitchen holding a dish towel I'd already folded twice. I hadn't meant to make him feel guilty. He was entitled to spend an evening with his friends, and I had no desire to become the kind of person who resented that.

What bothered me was harder to admit.

I wanted him to think of me without being reminded. I wanted, occasionally, to be the person he made plans around rather than the person he informed of them.

It felt embarrassingly childish when I put it that way.

So I didn't put it into words at all.

In the early years, he used to call me on his way home. Sometimes he had news, but more often there was no reason for the call. He'd describe a ridiculous conversation at work or something he'd seen on the train, and I'd tell him to stop distracting me and come home.

I never imagined those calls would become something I'd miss.

We still spoke every day, but our conversations had gradually become efficient. We knew what needed buying, which bills were due, and who would be home late. We remembered each other's schedules with the precision of people who had spent years coordinating a household.

He knew I disliked sugar in my coffee. I could tell from the way he closed the front door whether he'd had a difficult day. There was a familiarity between us that might have looked, to anyone else, like a particularly comfortable kind of love.

Perhaps it was.

Still, there were evenings when I would watch him talking animatedly to someone on the phone and realize I hadn't heard that particular laugh in weeks.

When he finished the call, I'd ask how his day had gone.

“Same as usual,” he'd say.

I had begun to accept that answer without asking anything further.

That Saturday, while looking through old photographs on my phone, I found one taken outside a noodle shop several years earlier.

We were standing in the rain. His umbrella had turned inside out in the wind, and I was laughing so hard that the photograph caught me with my eyes closed and my mouth wide open. His hair was plastered to his forehead. He looked thoroughly annoyed, though I remembered how hard he'd been laughing a moment later.

We'd missed the last bus that night.

I remembered that, too. We'd walked farther than we intended, talking about things that seemed terribly important then and that I could no longer recall.

It was strange, remembering the feeling of an evening more clearly than anything we'd said.

I studied the photograph for a while. It wasn't that we looked happier, exactly. We must have had worries then, too. Bills we couldn't quite afford, work we complained about, plans that didn't go the way we hoped.

But I remembered how easily we had occupied each other's attention.

Somewhere between that evening and this one, our conversations had grown shorter. We'd postponed plans and let small disappointments pass because there always seemed to be something more pressing. Eventually, we stopped expecting much from the hours we had together.

I put the phone down.

I missed him, although I wasn't entirely sure which version of him I missed.

He came home a little after eleven and set his keys quietly in the bowl by the door. It was a small consideration, one of many I had grown accustomed to receiving without noticing.

I was still on the sofa.

“Couldn't sleep?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

He sat beside me and switched on the television. I watched it with him, though afterward I couldn't have said what was on.

After a few minutes, he lowered the volume.

“Are you upset about tonight?”

I nearly told him I wasn't. It would have been easier, and I was tired.

“Not really,” I said. Then, because I knew how that sounded, I added, “It's not just tonight.”

He turned toward me.

“What is it, then?”

I tried to explain, but the words seemed either too dramatic or too small for what I meant.

“I think I've been lonely.”

He looked genuinely puzzled.

“But I'm here. I'm home every night.”

“I know.”

“Then I don't understand.”

Neither did I, entirely. That was part of the difficulty. I wasn't accusing him of abandoning me. He came home, remembered what I needed from the shops, fixed things before I noticed they were broken. There was evidence of his care everywhere I looked.

I just wasn't sure when we'd stopped enjoying each other.

“Sometimes,” I said, “it feels like all we do is take care of the house.”

He leaned back against the sofa.

“I'm trying to keep things going. You know that, right?”

“I do.”

“Then what am I supposed to do?”

I didn't have an answer ready. Until that evening, I'd barely acknowledged what I was feeling, let alone considered what might make it better.

“I miss spending time with you,” I said.

“We spend time together.”

“I know. I just mean…” I glanced at the television. “I don't know. I feel like we don't really talk anymore.”

He looked toward the screen, now playing almost silently.

For a moment, I regretted bringing it up. I could already imagine the next morning, the careful politeness that sometimes followed an argument neither of us wanted to resume.

Then he said, “I used to ask you to go places.”

I turned toward him.

“You remember that?”

“Of course I remember.”

He reminded me of a film he'd wanted to see and a weekend trip we'd discussed months earlier. There had been dinners, too, and invitations I'd put off because I was exhausted or because something at home needed finishing.

I remembered enough of them to feel uncomfortable.

“I was tired,” I said.

“I know you were.”

“I wasn't saying I didn't want to go with you.”

He rubbed his forehead.

“Well, after a while, that's what it felt like.”

I wanted to defend myself. There had been reasons for every refusal, and most of them had been good ones. But I could also remember the ease with which I'd said, Maybe another time, certain there would always be another invitation.

“You could have kept asking,” I said, and heard how unfair it sounded before he even answered.

He gave a small, tired laugh.

“How many times?”

I looked down at my hands.

We were quiet for a while.

I hadn't known he'd been carrying those disappointments. I could see the invitations I'd brushed aside, but I could also remember the evenings when he'd barely looked up from his phone as I tried to tell him something.

Neither memory cancelled the other.

Eventually, he reached for the remote and turned off the television.

“I'm sorry,” he said. “I didn't realize you felt like that.”

I nodded.

“I didn't realize you did, either.”

We talked for another hour.

Some of it was uncomfortable. We interrupted each other, disagreed about things that had happened months earlier, and occasionally argued over details neither of us could prove.

At one point, I cried, which frustrated me because I hadn't intended to. He went to get me a tissue and returned with the entire box.

I almost laughed.

After a while, he asked whether I still wanted us to do things together.

The question caught me off guard.

“Yes,” I said. “Of course.”

He nodded, but didn't say anything.

“Do you?” I asked.

He looked at me as though the answer should have been obvious.

Then he seemed to think better of it.

“Yeah,” he said. “I do.”

We went to bed without making promises.

I was grateful for that. We'd already made enough plans we hadn't kept.

The following afternoon, he asked if I wanted to go for a walk.

My first thought was the laundry. There were clothes in the machine and towels that needed hanging, and I'd hoped to get everything done before Monday.

I was about to explain this when I remembered our conversation.

“Give me ten minutes,” I said.

He was waiting by the door when I came out.

We walked without deciding where to go. For the first few streets, our conversation was awkward in the way it can be after you've told someone more than you intended. We discussed the weather, a new restaurant nearby, and a problem he'd been having at work.

Occasionally, we fell silent.

I kept thinking of things to say. He seemed content to walk.

At one point, I considered taking his arm, as I used to. I couldn't quite bring myself to do it, so I slipped my hands into my coat pockets instead.

At a crossing, he glanced toward a narrow shop on the opposite side of the street.

“Isn't that the bookstore you mentioned?”

I followed his gaze.

It was. I'd noticed the place several weeks earlier and told him I wanted to visit, though I'd forgotten about it since.

“I think so,” I said.

“Want to have a look?”

He was already watching the crossing signal. I don't think he noticed my surprise.

Inside, the shop was warmer than the street. There was a narrow table near the entrance stacked with secondhand novels, and somewhere toward the back a radio played quietly.

We wandered separately for a while.

I found him eventually near the travel section, absorbed in a book. He didn't look up when I approached, and for a moment I stood beside him, unsure whether to interrupt.

Then he turned the book toward me.

“Remember this place?”

It was about a country we'd once talked about visiting.

I looked through a few pages before giving it back.

“We never even booked the tickets,” I said.

He smiled.

“We weren't very good at the planning part.”

“No,” I said. “We weren't.”

He went back to the book for a moment, and I wandered toward a shelf of novels nearby.

A few minutes later, he came over.

“Ready?”

“Almost.”

He waited while I finished reading the back cover of a book I eventually put down.

We left without buying anything.

On the way out, he held the door for me. I thanked him, and he gave me an amused look, as though I'd become unusually formal.

For the first time that weekend, we both laughed.

Outside, the light had begun to fade.

We took the longer route home, partly because the weather was pleasant and partly because neither of us suggested turning back.

The laundry was still waiting, along with Monday and all the things we were so practiced at managing.

I wanted to ask him whether he thought things would be different now. But what could he have said? Neither of us knew what would happen when the ease of that afternoon wore off.

We were approaching our street when he slowed down to walk beside me.

For several steps, neither of us spoke.

Then he reached for my hand.

I took it.

And we kept walking.

An original work of fiction. The narrator and events are fictional, not an account of the artist’s personal life.
