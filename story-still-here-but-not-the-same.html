Still Here, But Not the Same

They still shared a life. She just couldn't remember the last time she felt chosen in it.

October 11, 2026A short story about distance and finding a way back

She couldn't remember the last time he had asked her to come along.

Not to the grocery store or to pick up something for the house. Somewhere he actually wanted to go. Just the two of them.

They still lived together. He remembered her coffee order. She knew which nights he came home late. They texted about dinner, paid the bills, and asked each other to lock the door.

Nothing was obviously wrong. That was what made it so difficult to explain.

There had been a time when he would call her on his way home just to tell her something funny. Now she often heard about his day while he was telling someone else about it. When she asked how work had gone, he usually said, “Same as always.”

She tried not to turn every small change into evidence of something bigger. People got tired. Life got busy. She was tired, too.

Still, she kept noticing things she wished she didn't.

One Saturday, he mentioned that he was meeting friends for dinner. He said it casually, while looking for his keys.

“Oh,” she said. “I thought we might do something tonight.”

He stopped. “You didn't say anything.”

“I know.”

He waited, but she didn't know how to finish the sentence. I wanted you to think of me sounded childish, even in her own head.

“We can go tomorrow,” he offered.

“It's okay. Have fun.”

She meant it, mostly. She didn't want him to cancel. She wanted the kind of closeness where she didn't always have to ask to be included.

Later, she sat alone at the kitchen table, scrolling past a photo of the two of them from a few years earlier. They were laughing at something outside the frame. She couldn't remember the joke. She could remember how easy it had been to laugh together.

When he got home, she was still awake.

“Are you mad?” he asked.

“No.” Then, after a moment: “I think I've been lonely.”

He frowned. “I'm here every day.”

“I know. That's why I haven't known what to say.”

He sat down across from her. At first, he defended himself. He was working hard. He hadn't gone anywhere. He thought they were doing fine. She said she knew he was trying, but she was tired of feeling like a housemate who happened to know everything about him.

That hurt him. She could see it. Some of what he said hurt her, too.

Then he admitted something quieter: lately, every conversation between them seemed to be about what needed doing. He had stopped suggesting things because he assumed they were both too exhausted. She hadn't realized how often she'd answered his old invitations with “Maybe next time.”

It didn't erase her loneliness. It didn't mean she'd imagined the distance. But for the first time, they could see how they had reached the same room from different sides.

They didn't solve it that night.

On Sunday, he asked if she wanted to take a walk. She almost said she had laundry to finish. Instead, she left it for later.

They walked around the neighborhood, talking about ordinary things. At one point they fell silent, and she wondered whether they were trying too hard.

Then he asked her a question about something she'd mentioned weeks ago. He remembered. She hadn't expected that to matter so much.

It was one walk. There were still years of habits between them, and neither knew whether good intentions would survive another busy week.

But when they reached home, she didn't feel exactly as she had the day before.

They were still here. They weren't the same. And maybe the next part of their story would depend less on what they once had, and more on what they chose to do now.
