The Things We Couldn't Promise

They understood what they felt. They also understood what love could not ask either of them to give up.

November 16, 2026Original short fiction by CHARISH JOY

The map was too large for the café table.

Mara had unfolded it anyway, smoothing the creases with the side of her hand while Daniel rescued their cups before the paper knocked them over.

“You could just use your phone,” he said.

“Then I wouldn’t get to do this.”

She circled what she thought was the road from her new apartment to the hospital where she would begin work in January. The estate agent had called it a short walk, though it looked farther on paper.

Daniel leaned over the map and turned it the right way up.

“You’ve circled a river.”

She looked closer. He was right.

They laughed loudly enough for the woman at the next table to glance over.

It was their last Thursday before Mara left. They had been meeting at this café for nearly four years, usually after work, usually without making a plan. She had been offered a position in another country that she’d once spoken about so often he could recite the application requirements from memory.

When the offer finally came, Daniel was the first person she called.

He had shouted into the phone, “You got it!”

She remembered being pleased that he sounded so happy.

She also remembered wishing, for one foolish second, that he might ask her not to go.

“I found a grocery store,” he said, pointing to a building beside the river she had mistaken for a street. “You’ll survive.”

“That was the main concern.”

He picked up his coffee. She noticed the small burn on his thumb, earned earlier that week while fixing a faulty kettle at his father’s shop.

His father had been struggling to keep the place open since a recent operation. Daniel had taken over the accounts, the early deliveries, and most of the evenings. There was no dramatic speech about duty. He simply arrived when he was needed.

Once, in the middle of summer, Mara had suggested a weekend away. Daniel looked at his calendar and said he couldn’t.

“Another time,” she’d answered.

They both knew how often they said that.

There had been other almost-plans. A concert they never booked. A trip to the coast they talked about until summer ended.

Once, after a late dinner, Mara had pulled up train schedules. Daniel asked her to send him the details.

She never did.

Neither of them brought it up again.

At seven, the café staff began stacking chairs near the back. Mara folded the map along the wrong crease, then tried again. Daniel held one corner while she worked the others into place.

“Are you frightened?” he asked.

“About the new job?”

“About all of it.”

She could have said no. Instead, she told him about the forms she was scared of filling out incorrectly, the unfamiliar streets, the possibility of sitting alone in a room after a difficult shift with no one nearby who knew her.

Daniel listened without offering a solution.

“You should still go,” he said when she finished.

“I know.”

“You worked hard for this.”

“I know that too.”

She looked at him, wondering whether he would say anything else.

He didn’t.

A chair scraped across the floor. The barista called a gentle warning that they were closing soon.

Mara slipped the map into its envelope. She had bought two identical pens that afternoon and now took one from her bag.

“For your impossible handwriting,” she said, setting it beside his cup.

Daniel turned it over in his fingers.

“It writes in blue.”

“I noticed.”

He smiled but didn’t put it away.

Outside, Mara stopped beside a bench to find her train card, setting the envelope down while she searched through her bag.

Daniel waited a step behind her, still holding the blue pen.

Then they continued toward the crossing where they usually separated, each waiting for a different signal.

“We could try,” Daniel said.

She knew what he meant. It was not the first time the possibility had appeared between them, though neither had given it a name.

For a moment, she imagined it working. Phone calls after her shifts. Visits planned months ahead. Daniel waiting at an airport with that familiar smile.

She wanted to believe they could manage it.

Her answer arrived more slowly than she wanted.

“Try what, exactly?”

He looked down at the pen in his hand.

“Calling. Visiting when we can.”

“And if I start wanting you to come back with me?”

“Mara—”

“And if you can’t?”

He said nothing.

He didn’t need to remind her about the shop, his father, the life he had built here. She didn’t need to explain why turning down the job would be more than a romantic gesture.

She wanted him to ask anyway.

That was the unfair part.

The pedestrian signal changed. Neither moved.

“I do care about you,” he said.

“I know.”

“I think you know more than that.”

She looked at him. There was no surprise left to perform.

“Yes,” she said. “I do.”

Across the street, a bus pulled away from the curb. Mara watched its taillights disappear while neither of them moved.

Daniel slipped the pen into his coat pocket.

“I don’t want you waiting for me,” he said.

“And I don’t want you promising something just because I’m leaving.”

It hurt to hear how easily they understood each other.

For a moment, she wanted to take it back. Tell him she could wait, that they could make promises anyway, that people worked through harder things.

But she had seen how tired he was lately. He had seen how much this opportunity meant to her.

Neither wanted to become the reason the other felt guilty for choosing a life they needed to live.

When the next signal changed, Mara stepped forward. Daniel walked beside her as far as the other pavement.

At the corner, he handed her the map.

She hadn’t noticed him picking it up from the bench.

“You’ll need this,” he said.

“It’s mostly wrong.”

“You can correct it when you get there.”

She laughed, and for a moment they were just two people who had spent years making each other laugh.

Then she tucked the envelope under her arm and went toward the station.

Later, on the train, she opened it once more. In the corner, he had written a single line in blue ink:

“Send me a picture when you find the real river.”

She traced the words with her thumb.

She could do that, she thought. Send a photograph. Tell him she had arrived.

She almost took out her phone to ask whether he had made it home.

Instead, she looked at the empty seat beside her.

They would still have things to tell each other. She knew that. What she didn’t know was how long they could keep sharing the ordinary details of their lives without wishing for more.

She carefully folded the map and put it away.

Tonight, it was enough to know that both of them had meant what they said.

She wasn’t sure it would feel like enough tomorrow.

An original work of fiction inspired by the song “We Both Know.” Characters and events are imagined, not an account of the artist’s personal life.
